## Chapter 1 — Origin: an operator, not a guru

Leanta was started by Samuel Vyhnanek in Ireland — someone whose working background is real production and hospitality operations, not marketing. The name comes from the Irish *lean* — to continue, to follow through.

The origin is unglamorous: an operator on a production floor, watching paperwork eat the evenings — temperature logs, training records, traceability sheets, rosters — and automating his own first. The records didn't get less legally required; they got faster.

The insight that became the business: small businesses everywhere drown in the **same seven routine pains** — documentation, training records, rosters, accounting prep, stock paperwork, sales admin, reporting. Different counters, same drowning. A café in Cork, a B&B in Mayo and a food producer in Žilina are filling in structurally identical forms, badly, late, in the hour they should be sleeping. (The agency offer later grew to nine service lines, but it started as these seven.)

So Leanta does two things from day one: **sells the workbooks** (so an owner can fix it themselves tonight, €15–69, one-time) and **does it with them** (Lean Six Sigma + AI automation, on-site, data-safety-first).

**What we'd teach:**
- Scratch your own itch in a domain where you hold operating credibility — it shows in every sentence of copy.
- The pains worth productising are *routine, repeated and legally compelled* — not interesting ones.
- Decide the two-sided model (product + service) early; each side feeds the other.

**Discussion questions:**
1. Which recurring task in your own work would you automate first, and what's the legal or financial driver behind it?
2. Why does "operator credibility" matter more in compliance than in most niches?

## Chapter 2 — The intelligence phase

Before building anything, a research pipeline ran across **13 sessions**, cataloguing **115 candidate assets across 4 verticals**. Every asset was scored with a defensible rubric:

> **Legal ×3 + Revenue ×2 + Pain ×2 + Frequency**

Legal compulsion weighs triple because a legally required record is bought under a different psychology than a nice-to-have — the buyer isn't persuaded, they're *due*. Revenue and pain weigh double; frequency catches the daily-use assets that build habit.

The phase was **evidence-first**: an asset only scored if it had a basis in published requirements and live market comparables (what's actually listed and selling on Etsy/Gumroad). The output was a ranked build queue stored in the internal research base — not a brainstorm, a ledger.

Hospitality won the queue: inspection cadence, statutory records, owner-operators with cards in hand.

**What we'd teach:**
- Score ideas with a written rubric *before* falling in love with any of them; weight the rubric by buying psychology, not personal interest.
- "Evidence-first" means every product traces to a requirement and a comparable — if you can't cite either, it doesn't enter the queue.
- 13 sessions of research is cheap against months of building the wrong thing.

**Discussion questions:**
1. Build a 4-factor rubric for your own niche. What deserves the ×3?
2. What's the failure mode of skipping comparables research, and how would you detect it early?

## Chapter 3 — Product decisions

- **Hospitality first.** Highest rubric scores: legal compulsion + inspection cadence + impulse-priced fixes.
- **Bilingual EN/SK as the wedge.** Every sheet labelled in English and Slovak. Cheap to build in from day one, near-impossible for template-flippers to retrofit credibly, and it opens the Slovak/CEE secondary market without a second product line.
- **The value ladder:** free (10-question readiness quiz + watermarked demo workbooks) → module (€15–24: Fire Safety Register, Staff Training Matrix, Safety Statement Builder, Cashflow & P&L Tracker) → pack (€29–49: Readiness Check, Compliance Pack, Operations & GP Bundle) → kit (Hospitality Pro Bundle €69 — a genuine €14 sum-of-parts saving, €34 + €49 = €83).
- **Omnibus-safe honest marketing as a design constraint, not a compliance chore:** no fake timers, no manufactured scarcity, no invented "was" prices; templates clearly framed as *not legal advice*; "audit-ready for a TÜV-style certifier; not affiliated" — never "TÜV template"; "BI-grade clarity without BI-grade cost" — never a claim to beat Power BI/Tableau/Datarails. The constraint simplified the copy: when you can't use tricks, you describe the product.

**What we'd teach:**
- A value ladder lets strangers say yes at €0 and customers say yes again at €69 — design the whole ladder before launching the first rung.
- Differentiators that are *structural* (bilingual sheets, live formulas) beat differentiators that are *claims*.
- Treat consumer-protection law as a copy-editor: everything it strikes out was weakening trust anyway.

**Discussion questions:**
1. Where on your ladder does trust transfer from free to paid, and what earns it?
2. Which of your current marketing claims would survive an Omnibus audit verbatim?

## Chapter 4 — Platform & money

- **Merchant of Record decision:** Lemon Squeezy as primary checkout. As MoR it handles EU VAT entirely — for a solo founder selling cross-border digital goods, that converts a week of VAT research and ongoing filings into zero admin. Gumroad stands by as fallback; Etsy serves as the discovery channel (search intent: people type "HACCP template" with intent to buy), with a static-file delivery workaround documented in `marketing/listings_etsy_gumroad.md`.
- **Pricing from live comparables**, not vibes: bands were set from what comparable compliance/operations templates actually list and sell for. One-time purchase, single-business licence, free updates when standards change.
- **The €29 launch-test decision:** the Compliance Readiness Check launches at €29, with a planned €19/€29/€39 experiment (§41 of the pricing research) — sequential bands, decided on revenue per 100 visitors, with a hard floor (never sell below €19) and no discount theatre.
- Until checkout URLs go live, site buy buttons collect email pre-orders to hello@leanta.ie — manual fulfilment, logged.

**What we'd teach:**
- For EU digital sales, the MoR-vs-own-checkout decision is a time decision, not just a fees decision.
- Price tests need fixed windows, one variable, and a pre-committed metric — otherwise they're anecdotes.
- A pre-order mode means launch day never blocks on a payment processor.

**Discussion questions:**
1. Compute the real cost of self-handled EU VAT for a one-person shop. At what scale does MoR stop being worth its fee?
2. Why decide the price experiment's success metric *before* seeing any data?

**What we'd teach:**
- A launch is a checklist, not a mood — Tuesday-to-Friday, every item owned and dated.
- AI agents compress build time only when a human owns final review; speed without review is liability, especially in compliance.
- Ship security hardening *before* marketing, not after the first leak.

## Chapter 6 — Lessons so far (living chapter)

Recorded as they actually happen. Pre-launch lessons, honestly held:

1. **The rubric prevented flattering products.** Several "fun" ideas scored poorly on Legal×3 and died on paper instead of in the market.
2. **The MoR choice removed a whole category of dread.** EU VAT stopped being a reason to delay.
3. **Bilingual is nearly free at build time and brutal to retrofit** — the wedge cost almost nothing because it was a day-one decision.
4. **Honest-marketing constraints simplified everything.** With tricks off the table, every page just describes the product and its price.
